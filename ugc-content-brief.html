How to brief a UGC creator (copy-paste template included)

A great UGC brief gives a creator four things on a single page: the product truth in plain language, the audience's real pain point, two or three hook directions to test, and a short list of mandatories and never-says. It deliberately stops short of a script — brief the hook, let the creator own the delivery. Anything longer than a page stops being a brief and starts being an ad read, and the feed can tell.

What should a UGC brief include?

Seven things, and it should still fit on one page:

  • Product truth. What the product actually does and the one claim that matters most. Not your website copy — the sentence a happy customer would say to a friend. Creators can't sell what they don't understand, and vague briefs produce vague videos.
  • Audience and pain points. Who buys, and what problem pushes them to buy. "Busy parents who quit meal-kit boxes over the packaging waste" briefs better than a demographics table ever will.
  • Two or three hook directions. The first seconds decide whether a video gets watched at all — that's how short-form reach works — so the hook is the one part of the video a brand should shape. More than three directions per round dilutes the test.
  • Mandatories. The short list of what must appear: the product name pronounced correctly, the feature shown on screen, the disclaimer if you're in a regulated niche.
  • What NOT to say. Banned claims, competitor names, words legal hasn't cleared. This is the section that prevents expensive reshoots — write it even when it feels obvious.
  • Format and length. Platform, target length, and style: talking-head review, demo, POV skit, voiceover. Our breakdown of UGC formats that convert is a menu to pick from.
  • Example posts. Two or three links to videos in the tone you're after. "Like this, but for our product" is the fastest brief ever written.

One thing that deliberately isn't on the list: usage rights. Who can repost the video, whether it can run as an ad, and for how long are contract questions, not creative ones — UGC usage rights explained covers what to put in writing before the shoot.

The copy-paste UGC brief template

Copy this into a doc, fill in the blanks, and keep the finished version under one page. If a section fights you, that usually means you don't know the answer yet — fix that before hiring anyone.

  1. Product truth. What it is and what it does, in one sentence a customer would actually say. The single thing a viewer should remember: ___
  2. Audience. Who buys it: ___ The pain point that drives them, in their own words: ___
  3. Hook directions (pick one per video). Hook A: ___ Hook B: ___ Hook C: ___
  4. Mandatories. Must appear or be said: ___ (product name, the feature on screen, any required disclaimer)
  5. Never say. Claims and words that are off-limits: ___ (medical or income promises, competitor names, anything unapproved)
  6. Format. Platform: ___ Length: ___ seconds. Style: talking-head / demo / POV / voiceover.
  7. Reference posts. Two or three links to videos in the style you want: ___
  8. Deliverables and deadline. ___ videos by ___, delivered as ___ (posted to the creator's account, or files handed over).

Hook directions are angles, not lines: "the skeptic who got converted," "three things I wish I knew before I bought," "I replaced [old habit] with this." Each one gives a creator a doorway into the video without dictating the words. If you catch yourself writing full sentences for the creator to repeat, you've crossed from hook direction into script — and the next section is about why that costs you.

Should you script UGC creators word for word?

No. Over-scripting is the most common briefing mistake and the most expensive one, because it defeats the reason you hired a creator in the first place. Scripted UGC has a tell — the flat cadence of someone reading — and viewers treat it exactly like the ads they already scroll past. The creator's own phrasing, pacing, and slang are the asset you're buying. Strip them out and you've paid creator rates for a worse-produced commercial.

Our production rule is one line: brief the hook, let the creator own the delivery. The hook is strategy — it's testable, and comparable across creators. Everything after the hook is performance, and performance is the creator's craft. When we ran Medceptor's campaign, 10 creators produced 300 unique videos in 30 days, published as 1,200 posts across the team's accounts. Nobody can script 300 videos in a month, and nobody should try: the campaign reached 4.1 million views and a +26% revenue lift precisely because ten different voices interpreted the same hook directions ten different ways. Full numbers are in our case studies.

The honest tradeoff: giving up the script means you'll occasionally get a video you'd never have written — a rambling intro, a joke that doesn't land. That's the cost. The return is content the feed can't distinguish from a real person's post, because it is one. A brand that can't tolerate a single off-brand frame should buy studio ads, not UGC.

How do you iterate after week one?

The first brief is a hypothesis, not scripture. After a week of posting, the data answers three questions:

  • Which hook direction earned watch-time? Compare average watch-through across hooks, not raw views — a small sample of view counts is mostly noise.
  • Which format earned engagement? Saves and shares are stronger buy signals than likes. If demos get shared and skits don't, the next brief leans demo.
  • What did the comments correct? A mispronounced name, a price question nobody answered, an objection that keeps recurring — next week's mandatories and hooks come straight from the comment section.

Then rewrite: keep the winning hook and brief two variations of it, kill the weakest, add one fresh direction. Weekly is the right cadence — faster and you're reacting to noise, slower and you're wasting posts on a stale brief. If nothing wins, the problem is usually the product-truth section: you briefed marketing copy instead of a customer's sentence. Our Memo campaign is what the loop looks like compounding — 145 posts and 4.29 million views from an account with 2,672 followers, at a 3.49% tracked conversion rate. That's iteration on a working format, not one lucky video.

A sharp brief gets you a better video.
